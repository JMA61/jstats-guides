One-way ANOVA

This page is a hands-on run-through of a one-way ANOVA with jaov(): which version of the test to run, how to check the assumption that decides between the two versions, which follow-up comparisons go with each, and what to put in your write-up.

A one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) compares the average of a numeric outcome across the categories of one categorical variable — usually three or more groups. The page assumes you have met the test in a course or a textbook, and concentrates on running the test and reporting it.

This page follows the pattern of every How-To: numbered blocks that build one script, with the finished script to download at the bottom. Using the How-To pages explains the pattern, and how to reset your session if something goes wrong.

1. Load jstats and choose the data

We’ll work on clinic, one of the two practice datasets that ship with the package: 70 simulated clients in a study of four treatment conditions — a control group, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness training, and a support group. juse() makes clinic the default data frame, so the commands that follow can leave its name out.

## ---- 1. Load jstats and choose the data -------------------------------

## Load jstats for this session, then make the clinic practice dataset
## the default, so the commands below can leave its name out
library(jstats)
juse(clinic)

The command lines are shown; the standalone ## comment lines aren’t – running a comment just echoes it back and does nothing.

2. Run the standard ANOVA

The outcome is Flourishing, a wellbeing score from 0 to 100, and the grouping variable is Condition. jaov() takes a formula, with the outcome on the left of the tilde (~) and the grouping variable on the right.

## ---- 2. Run the standard ANOVA ----------------------------------------

## Is there evidence that average flourishing differs across the four
## treatment conditions? The outcome goes left of the ~, the groups right
jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition)

The result is in the first row of the ANOVA table. The p is below .05, the usual threshold, so the test is statistically significant: evidence that average flourishing differs across the four conditions in the population from which the sample was selected, and not only in the sample.

TipIf you’re new to statistics

The four group means in the output differ; in real data they always do. That is not the question. These 70 clients are a sample, and the question is about the population from which the sample was selected: if the four conditions made no difference in that population, could differences this size have turned up in the sample simply through the luck of who was selected? The ANOVA is a test of exactly that. (With data on a whole population, a census, there would be nothing to test. The means you see would simply be the population’s means.)

NoteReading this output
  • The title names the test. “One-Way ANOVA” is the standard version. The line under the title confirms which data frame was used, since the command left the data frame’s name out.
  • Analysis N is the number of cases the test used — all 70 here, because neither variable has missing values. When cases are set aside, a small Case Processing table takes this line’s place and says how many and why (block 9 has one).
  • Group Descriptives gives each group’s size, mean, and standard deviation, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean. Look here first: these are the differences in your sample.
  • The ANOVA table is the test itself. Two numbers in its first row carry the result, F and p. The larger the F and the smaller the p, the stronger the evidence that the means differ in the population and not only in the sample. By the usual convention, a p below .05 counts as statistically significant. A significant result does not say the difference is large or important, and a non-significant result does not show the groups are the same in the population. The rest of the table, and the Eta-squared line under it, are covered in the books and in most statistics courses. You will need some of those numbers for a write-up; the last section of this page shows where to find them.
NoteWhy jstats does it this way

One function runs both versions of this test, the standard ANOVA and Welch’s ANOVA, and one argument, welch = TRUE, switches between them. The same argument does the same job in jstats’s t-test, jt(). Both functions default to the standard version, so a t-test and an ANOVA on the same two groups give the same answer.

Base R arranges things differently. Its standard ANOVA is aov(); Welch’s version is a separate function, oneway.test(), with different output. Base R’s t-test, t.test(), runs Welch’s version unless told otherwise, while aov() runs the standard version, so on the same two groups the two functions give different answers until you change a default. Levene’s test and the Games-Howell comparisons, both used later on this page, are not in base R at all. Add-on packages fill those gaps, each with its own syntax, defaults, and output.

Base R also has to be told that the grouping variable is categorical. Condition holds the codes 1 to 4 with labels attached, which is how data from commercial statistical software usually arrive. Hand Condition to aov() as it is, and R treats the codes as a measured quantity and runs a different analysis, with no warning. jaov() reads the labels, makes the conversion for you, and then runs base R’s own aov() underneath — the same proven code, with the setup done.

3. The other version, Welch’s ANOVA

jstats offers two versions of the test. The standard ANOVA you just ran assumes that the scores are about equally spread out in every group — equal variances, also called homogeneity of variance. Welch’s ANOVA drops that assumption.

The standard version is the default because it is the traditional one: the test most courses and textbooks teach, the table commercial statistical software prints first, and the one most readers of your work will expect unless you say otherwise. Welch’s version is one argument away: welch = TRUE.

## ---- 3. The other version, Welch's ANOVA ------------------------------

## The same question, without assuming the groups are equally spread out
jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition, welch = TRUE)

Welch’s ANOVA is significant too, so on these data the two versions lead to the same conclusion. The two versions will not always agree, and the next two blocks are about choosing between them.

NoteReading this output

The title now says Welch’s. The test table is shorter than the standard one: it shows F, two degrees-of-freedom values, and p, and the note under the table says that sums of squares and mean squares do not apply to Welch’s ANOVA. The second degrees-of-freedom value carries a decimal, which is normal for this test. F and p are read as in block 2. The Group Descriptives table is the same as in block 2, because Welch’s ANOVA changes how the means are tested, not the means. The Eta-squared line has a note of its own, because the effect size is still worked out the standard way.

NoteGoing deeper (optional)

Some statisticians now recommend running Welch’s version as a matter of routine, on the grounds that it costs little when the variances are equal and protects you when the variances are not. Others, and most courses, keep the standard test unless there is a reason to leave it. Either position is defensible. What matters is that you say which test you ran and why, and that you follow the preference of your field, or of whoever will assess your work, where there is one. The help page ?jaov documents every option used on this page; Book 1 covers what the F test is doing and why unequal variances matter, in more detail, with worked examples.

4. Check equal variances with Levene’s test

Whether the variances are equal is something you can check. Levene’s test compares the spread of the groups, and levene = TRUE adds Levene’s test to the output.

## ---- 4. Check equal variances with Levene's test ----------------------

## Levene's test asks whether the groups differ in spread. It is off by
## default, so ask for it
jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition, levene = TRUE)

Levene’s test is not significant here: its p is above .05. The test found no evidence that the four conditions differ in spread in the population, so there is no reason to doubt the standard ANOVA’s assumption, and the standard ANOVA from block 2 is the one to report.

Equal variances is the assumption most people have in mind when they talk about checking an ANOVA, and it is the assumption that decides between the two versions of the test. Both versions make two other assumptions as well: that the cases are independent of one another, and that the scores within each group are roughly normally distributed. The box plot in block 8 gives a first look at the second of those.

NoteReading this output

The new table sits at the top, above the descriptives. Levene’s test has an F and a p of its own, and its p is read the other way around from most tests: a p above .05 is the reassuring result. When Levene’s test is not significant, jstats adds nothing further, and the rest of the output is what block 2 printed.

Glance at the SD column of Group Descriptives as well. Levene’s test is a guide, not a verdict: with small groups the test can miss a real difference in spread, and with very large groups the test can flag a trivial one. Here the Control group’s SD is the largest of the four, though not by enough for Levene’s test to flag it; block 8 shows where that extra spread comes from.

5. When Levene’s test is significant

To see the other outcome of Levene’s test we need a different variable. ScreenTime is each client’s daily screen time, in hours.

This time Levene’s test is significant: evidence that the four conditions differ in spread. jstats adds a note under the Levene table, and because the four groups are close to the same size, the note says the standard ANOVA remains appropriate.

That gives the conventional rule for choosing between the two versions:

  • Levene’s test not significant: report the standard ANOVA.
  • Levene’s test significant, group sizes about equal: the standard ANOVA is usually still acceptable. Welch’s ANOVA is the safer choice when the SDs are far apart, as some of the SDs are here. Say which version you used.
  • Levene’s test significant, group sizes unequal: use Welch’s ANOVA.

The rule is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee. Whichever version you choose, say what you did and why.

NoteReading this output

Levene’s p is below .05, and the SD column shows why: the Control group’s screen time is bunched more tightly than the other groups’.

The note under the Levene table is there because unequal variances matter more in some situations than in others. The standard ANOVA is least affected by unequal variances when the groups are about the same size, and most affected when the group sizes are unequal as well. So jstats checks the group sizes for you. jstats treats the group sizes as approximately equal when the largest group is no more than 1.5 times the size of the smallest, a rule of thumb. Here the largest group has 21 clients and the smallest has 14, which is exactly 1.5. Had the group sizes been further apart, the note would have said instead that the unequal variances may affect the results, and suggested welch = TRUE.

The ANOVA itself is not significant in this output: the test gives no evidence that average screen time differs across the conditions in the population. Levene’s test and the ANOVA answer different questions, one about spread and one about means.

6. Which groups differ, Tukey HSD

Back to Flourishing. A significant ANOVA is evidence that the group means are not all the same in the population. A significant ANOVA does not say which groups differ. That is the job of post-hoc comparisons: every pair of groups compared, with the p-values adjusted so that making several comparisons at once does not inflate the chance of a false alarm. posthoc = TRUE adds the comparisons, and after the standard ANOVA the method is Tukey HSD (the letters stand for “honestly significant difference”).

## ---- 6. Which groups differ, Tukey HSD --------------------------------

## Post-hoc comparisons test every pair of groups. After the standard
## ANOVA, the method is Tukey HSD
jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition, posthoc = TRUE)

NoteReading this output

The new table is at the bottom. Each row is one pair of groups, and the row’s name says which way the subtraction runs: CBT-Control is the CBT mean minus the Control mean, so a positive difference means CBT scored higher. Next comes the 95% confidence interval for that difference, and then the adjusted p. Because the adjustment is built in, you read each p against .05 as it stands. The interval tells the same story: an interval that does not include zero goes with a p below .05.

Four groups make six pairs, and five groups would make ten, so this table grows quickly. If the table’s lines wrap in your Console, drag the Console pane wider and run the line again.

7. Which groups differ after Welch’s, Games-Howell

Tukey HSD carries the same equal-variances assumption as the standard ANOVA. If you used Welch’s ANOVA, the matching method is Games-Howell, which does not assume equal variances either. You don’t ask for Games-Howell by name: posthoc = TRUE gives Tukey HSD after the standard ANOVA and Games-Howell after Welch’s ANOVA.

## ---- 7. Which groups differ after Welch's, Games-Howell ---------------

## The same request after Welch's ANOVA gives Games-Howell comparisons,
## which do not assume equal variances either
jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition, welch = TRUE, posthoc = TRUE)

NoteReading this output

The table has the same layout as the Tukey HSD table, with one more column, df. Games-Howell judges each pair on those two groups’ own variances, so each comparison has a degrees-of-freedom value of its own. You need the df column only if your write-up reports it. The mean differences are the same as in the Tukey HSD table, because they are simply differences between group means. The intervals and, as a rule, the p-values are not the same.

One caution. Games-Howell has to estimate each group’s variance from that group alone, and with very small groups its p-values can come out too small. A rule of thumb you will see quoted is at least six cases in every group.

8. Look at the groups with jplot()

A table of means summarizes each group in a few numbers; a plot shows what the numbers are summarizing. Save the ANOVA’s result under a name with the assignment operator, <-, and hand that name to jplot().

## ---- 8. Look at the groups with jplot() -------------------------------

## Save the result under a name, then hand it to jplot() for a box plot
## of each group. The plot appears in the Plots tab (lower-right pane)
Results <- jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition)
jplot(Results)

The first line prints the same output as block 2 while saving the result as Results. The second line draws a box plot of each group in the Plots tab of the lower-right pane:

One thing stands out: a single client in the Control group, far below everyone else, with a flourishing score of 0. A score like that deserves a look at the data before you rely on the result — is the score real, or a typing slip? (In these practice data the score is simply very low.)

NoteReading this output

Each box spans the middle half of that group’s scores, the heavy line across the box is the median, and the diamond marks the mean — the number the ANOVA compares. The lines above and below the box reach to the lowest and highest scores that are not unusually far from the box, and any score beyond those lines is drawn as a separate point. The horizontal axis shows each condition by its code; the Group Descriptives table has the names (1 is Control, 2 CBT, 3 Mindfulness, 4 Support group).

Two things are worth looking for, because either can distort a mean and a standard deviation. Skew: a median line well off the center of its box, or a diamond some distance from the median line, means the scores are stretched out in one direction. Outliers: the separate points. The Control client with a score of 0 drags the Control group’s mean down and stretches the Control group’s standard deviation; that one client is the main reason Control’s SD is the largest in the Group Descriptives table.

9. A group with one case

Sometimes a category turns up with a single case in it: one respondent who checked “Other,” one site that recruited one participant. It is worth knowing what each version of the test does then. No group in clinic is that small, so this block makes one. The subset = argument keeps only some of the cases, for this one call, and the rule written here keeps every client who is not in CBT, plus one client who is.

## ---- 9. A group with one case -----------------------------------------

## No group in clinic is that small, so make one for this call only:
## subset = keeps every client who is not in CBT (condition 2), plus one
## who is (!= means "is not equal to", and | means "or")
jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition, posthoc = TRUE,
     subset = Condition != 2 | ClientID == "C016")

In the Tukey HSD table, the three rows that involve CBT have intervals far wider than the other rows.

10. One case under Welch’s ANOVA

Welch’s ANOVA cannot do the same. Welch’s ANOVA needs each group’s own variance, and a single case does not have one. Run the same analysis as a Welch’s ANOVA and jstats stops — the one deliberate error on this page.

## ---- 10. One case under Welch's ANOVA ---------------------------------

## The same cases as a Welch's ANOVA. It needs each group's own variance,
## and one case has none, so jstats stops and says so
jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition, welch = TRUE,  # an error, on purpose
     subset = Condition != 2 | ClientID == "C016")
Console (lower-left pane) 
ConsoleTerminal×Background Jobs×> jaov(Flourishing ~ Condition, welch = TRUE,  # an error, on purpose
+      subset = Condition != 2 | ClientID == "C016")
Welch's One-Way ANOVA
Using default data frame: clinic

Case Processing  Excluded  Remaining
    Original           --         70
    subset =           13         57  Condition != 2 | ClientID == "C016"
    Analysis N         --         57
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Error: jaov(): 'Condition' has 1 category with only 1 case (CBT).
Welch's ANOVA requires at least 2 cases in every category.
The standard ANOVA can include it: run jaov() without welch = TRUE.
> |

The error names the category, says what Welch’s ANOVA requires (at least two cases in every category), and gives the way out. Which way to take is a judgment about your study, not about the software: keep the one-case group and use the standard ANOVA, combine that group with a category it belongs with, or leave that group out. Whichever you choose, say so in your write-up.

11. Everything in one call

Once you know the pieces, full = TRUE asks for all of them together: Levene’s test, the confidence intervals, the effect size, and the post-hoc comparisons. Add welch = TRUE to the same call for the Welch route.

## ---- 11. Everything in one call ---------------------------------------

## full = TRUE asks for Levene's test, the confidence intervals, the

TipComing from SPSS, Stata, or SAS?

Everything on this page has a counterpart in the commercial programs, though the pieces are arranged differently in each.

In SPSS the tests are all in one dialog: One-Way ANOVA, in the Analyze menu under Compare Means (Compare Means and Proportions in recent versions). The Options button holds the descriptives, the homogeneity of variance test (Levene’s), and Welch’s test; the Post Hoc button holds Tukey and, in its “Equal Variances Not Assumed” group, Games-Howell. SPSS labels the rows of its ANOVA table Between Groups and Within Groups, where jstats names the grouping variable and Residual. In recent versions SPSS’s Levene table has several rows; the test jstats runs is the classic one, built on each score’s distance from its own group’s mean, which is the row SPSS labels “Based on Mean.”

In Stata, oneway Flourishing Condition, tabulate runs the standard ANOVA with the group means. The equal-variances test that command prints is Bartlett’s, a different test; Levene’s comes from robvar Flourishing, by(Condition), where it is the statistic labeled W0. Tukey comparisons are pwmean Flourishing, over(Condition) mcompare(tukey) effects. Welch’s ANOVA and Games-Howell are not part of those commands; community-contributed commands exist for both.

In SAS, a PROC GLM step with CLASS Condition; and MODEL Flourishing = Condition; runs the standard ANOVA, and one more statement, MEANS Condition / HOVTEST=LEVENE(TYPE=ABS) WELCH TUKEY;, adds Levene’s test, Welch’s ANOVA, and Tukey comparisons. The TYPE=ABS matters: left to its default, SAS’s Levene’s test works from squared distances and gives a different result from the one on this page. That MEANS statement has no Games-Howell option.

One thing jstats adds is the pairing: ask for post-hoc comparisons and you get the method that matches the test you ran.

What to write up

A results paragraph for a one-way ANOVA usually carries five things: which test you ran, the F with its two degrees-of-freedom values, the p, an effect size, and — when the test is significant — which groups differed, with their means and standard deviations. If you checked equal variances, say what you found: that check is the reason for your choice of test. The two examples below follow the general pattern of APA style, common in the social sciences. Conventions differ between fields and journals, so check what yours expects.

The standard route, from the output of blocks 4 and 6:

Levene’s test gave no indication of unequal variances, F(3, 66) = 0.79, p = .505, so a standard one-way ANOVA was used. Flourishing scores differed across the four treatment conditions, F(3, 66) = 4.65, p = .005, η² = .17. Tukey HSD comparisons showed higher scores in the CBT group (M = 56.14, SD = 11.11) and the Mindfulness group (M = 55.35, SD = 12.23) than in the Control group (M = 42.11, SD = 16.19), p = .017 for each; no other pair of conditions differed significantly.

The Welch route, from the output of block 7:

Welch’s ANOVA, which does not assume equal variances, showed that flourishing scores differed across the four treatment conditions, F(3, 35.1) = 4.23, p = .012. Games-Howell comparisons showed higher scores in the CBT group (M = 56.14, SD = 11.11) and the Mindfulness group (M = 55.35, SD = 12.23) than in the Control group (M = 42.11, SD = 16.19), p = .033 and p = .047 respectively; no other pair of conditions differed significantly.

Every number comes from the output. The statistics are rounded to two decimal places, a common convention; the p-values are left at three, and Welch’s second degrees-of-freedom value keeps the one decimal jstats prints. The two values in parentheses after F are its degrees of freedom: in the standard ANOVA table, the df of the first row and of the Residual row; in Welch’s table, df1 and df2. η² is the Eta-squared line. M and SD come from Group Descriptives, and the p-values of the comparisons come from the post-hoc table. A paragraph like these usually sits beside a table of every group’s mean and standard deviation, so the reader can see the groups the sentences leave out.

Two notes on the Welch example. In your own write-up, the first sentence would also give your reason for choosing Welch’s ANOVA, typically a significant Levene’s test with unequal group sizes. And the Welch example reports no effect size: the eta-squared that jstats prints under Welch’s table is worked out the standard way, as the note under it says, so if you report that eta-squared, say so.
